## Video 1 — structure and scannability

Verified chapter outline:

| 4:30 | Sponsor segment |

Practical interpretation supported by the attached examples:

- Align repeated information so the eye can scan a column. Dates and deadlines become easier to compare when their positions are stable.
- Use the outer edges deliberately: checkbox and task title on the left; deadline on the right. Avoid scattering metadata across each row.
- Density depends on structure, not simply the amount of content. Small section labels and consistent row spacing can organize a compact list.
- Give different information different visual treatments: links, people, categories, and time should be recognizable without reading every word.
- Keep the task title prominent. Secondary actions and labels should support it instead of competing with it.
- Design around what someone is looking for: the next task, its deadline, a linked file, or a person.

## Video 2 — perceived quality through behavior

| 3:40 | UI consistency |
| 4:27 | Empty states |

Working interpretation of those topics, pending narration verification:

- Put the likely next step within reach and preserve context as the user acts.
- Use transitions to explain changes of state or location.
- Add small moments of pleasure after useful actions, without slowing routine work.
- Keep frequent interactions quick; movement should have a purpose.
- Make spacing, icon style, control behavior, and feedback consistent across screens.
- Let empty states explain what belongs there and how to start.
- Make deeper capabilities discoverable while keeping the main path clear.

## Combined takeaway

My synthesis: visual quality comes from clear structure; perceived quality also depends on consistent, thoughtful behavior. Start with hierarchy and alignment, then refine contextual actions, feedback, transitions, and empty states. Do not use extra decoration as a substitute for those foundations.
